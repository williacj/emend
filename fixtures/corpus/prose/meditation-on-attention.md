# A Meditation on Attention

*In which attention is considered as a kind of labor, a kind of gift, and, in the
end, a kind of love.*

## The Thing We Spend Without Counting

Of all the things a person owns, attention is the one most freely spent and least
often counted. We guard our money and squander our notice. We would not hand a
stranger our purse, yet we hand the whole of our waking mind to whatever is
loudest in the room, and call it rest.

But attention is not rest. It is the most active thing we do, and everything else
we make depends on it. The carpenter's straight joint, the physician's right
diagnosis, the friend's timely word — each is attention made visible, hardened
into an outcome. Where attention has been thin, the joint is crooked, the
diagnosis late, the word unsaid until it is too late to matter.

> We become, in the long run, what we have attended to. The mind is not a vessel
> to be filled but a field to be tended, and a field grows what is planted in it,
> and nothing else.

## Attention as Labor

Begin with the hardest truth about attention: it is work, and it tires like work.
There is a reason the mind wanders, and the reason is not weakness of character.
The reason is that sustained attention is genuinely costly, and the mind, like any
economizing thing, looks for the cheaper path.

This has a practical consequence that the moralists usually miss. If attention is
labor, then it must be *rationed*, and the rationing is itself a skill. The person
who attends to everything attends well to nothing; he has spread a single lamp's
worth of oil across a hundred wicks and lit a hundred smokes. The disciplined mind
does the opposite. It chooses.

Consider the shape of a well-spent hour:

1. It begins with a decision — *this*, and not the hundred other things — and the
   decision is held, gently but firmly, against the tide of every interruption.
2. It endures a middle passage of tedium, where the first interest has faded and
   the reward has not yet arrived. This is where most hours are lost.
3. It arrives, if it is faithful, at a place the scattered hour never reaches: the
   place where the thing attended to begins, at last, to give something back.

The middle passage is the whole test. Anyone can begin. The gift is given only to
those who stay.

## Attention as a Gift to Others

Turn the lamp now from the work we do to the people we live among, and a second
face of attention appears. To attend to another person — really to attend, to hold
your own noise still long enough to hear theirs — is among the most generous
things one creature can do for another.

We know this from the receiving end. Everyone has felt the difference between being
*heard* and being merely *waited out*, between the eyes that are on you and the
eyes that are already on the door. The first makes us larger. The second, however
polite, makes us smaller, and we leave the encounter having somehow lost a little.

- To listen fully is to say, without a word: *you are worth the cost of my mind.*
- To listen half is to say the opposite, and to say it more loudly than any
  spoken insult, because the body does not know how to lie about where it is
  looking.
- To interrupt is not merely rude; it is a small theft, the taking back of a gift
  before it was fully given.

None of this requires eloquence. The most attentive people I have known were not
the most articulate. They had simply learned the rare art of being *present* on
purpose, and of letting it show.

## The Enemy Is Not Idleness

We are told the enemy of attention is idleness, but this is an old error and a
comfortable one. The idle mind, left alone, will eventually attend to something;
boredom is a hunger, and hunger finds food. The true enemy is not idleness but
*distraction* — the constant, gentle, colorful pull of the almost-interesting,
which never bores us enough to drive us toward the deeply interesting.

Distraction is dangerous precisely because it is pleasant. Pain we resist; pleasure
we drift into. And the drift is nearly invisible from the inside. No one decides to
waste a life. One only decides, ten thousand times, to attend to the nearer, easier,
brighter thing — and the ten thousand small decisions add up, without anyone's
consent, to the shape of a life.

> Guard the gate of the eye and the ear, not because the world beyond it is evil,
> but because it is *interesting*, and interest, ungoverned, will spend you to the
> last coin and leave you wondering where the day went.

## A Kind of Love

I have called attention a labor and a gift, and both are true. But there is a third
name for it, and it is the truest, though it will sound too large for so quiet a
thing. Attention, fully given, is a kind of love.

For what is love, in its plainest working form, but the refusal to look away? The
parent watching the sleeping child, the student bent over the difficult page, the
craftsman turning the piece for the twentieth time to catch the flaw the first
nineteen turns missed — each is doing the same thing, which is *staying*, which is
*not looking away*, which is the whole of the matter.

So attend, then, and count the spending. Give your notice as you would give your
money — deliberately, to things and people worth the price. The scattered life and
the gathered life are made of the very same hours. The only difference between
them is where the eyes were pointed, and whether anyone was home behind them.
