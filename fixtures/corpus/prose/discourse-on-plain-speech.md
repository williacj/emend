# A Discourse on Plain Speech

*Being an address on the plain use of words, and the debt a writer owes to those who read.*

## The Argument Stated

There is an old complaint, older than any of us, that words have grown cheap.
The complaint is half true and half lazy. Words are not cheap; they are simply
spent without thought, as a man spends coins in the dark and is surprised, come
morning, to find his purse empty and his errand undone. The remedy is not fewer
words but plainer ones. A plain word is a coin held up to the light before it is
laid down.

Let us begin, then, with a claim that will seem too simple to defend:

> The first duty a writer owes the reader is to be understood; the second is to
> be worth understanding. The order matters. A brilliant sentence that cannot be
> read is a lamp sealed in a box.

I have known writers who inverted this order, and they were, without exception,
admired and unread. Admiration is a poor wage. It buys nothing at the market of
actual minds, where the only currency is the thing understood and carried home.

## What Plainness Is Not

Plainness is easily mistaken for its cousins, and the mistake ruins good prose
before it is written. Let me name three impostors.

1. **Plainness is not poverty.** A plain style is not a starved one. The plainest
   writers in our language are also among the richest, because they spend their
   wealth on the reader instead of on themselves.
2. **Plainness is not flatness.** A flat sentence has no rise and no fall; a plain
   sentence may climb a considerable hill, so long as it takes the reader by the
   hand and does not let go on the way up.
3. **Plainness is not haste.** The plainest page is very often the slowest to
   write. It is easy to be obscure quickly. It is hard, and takes patience, to be
   clear.

Consider the difference between a fog and a clear pane of glass. Both are, in a
manner of speaking, colorless. But the fog hides what is behind it, and the glass
reveals it. A plain style is glass. You should be able to look *through* the
sentence at the thing it names, and forget, for a happy moment, that there was a
sentence there at all.

## The Reader Is Not a Stranger

Here is a thought worth keeping near at hand while you write: the reader is a
guest you have invited into your house. You would not, receiving a guest, hide
the chairs, mislabel the doors, and then take offense that he stumbled. Yet this
is precisely what obscure writing does. It invites, and then it hides the chairs.

The remedy is a kind of hospitality. Ask, of every hard sentence:

- Would a tired reader, reading this once, at the end of a long day, take my
  meaning on the first pass?
- Have I made him double back? If so, was the difficulty *in the matter*, which
  is honest, or *in my handling of it*, which is not?
- Have I used a long word where a short one would carry the same weight the same
  distance?

The tired reader is the truest judge. He has no patience to spare for your
cleverness and no incentive to flatter you. Write for him, and you will find you
have written for everyone.

## On the Fear of Being Simple

A young writer fears simplicity as a young soldier fears silence: he mistakes it
for the absence of something. He piles on the ornament because he suspects that
underneath the ornament there is nothing, and he is terrified that the reader
will discover the same.

But the discipline of plain speech is exactly the cure for this fear. When you
strip a thought down to its plainest words, one of two things happens, and both
are useful. Either the thought stands up on its own, and you may be glad of it;
or it falls down, and you have learned, cheaply and in private, that it was never
a thought at all but only a costume.

> Do not trust a sentence you cannot say aloud to a plain man and see him nod.
> The nod is the whole of the examination. Everything else is decoration on a
> door that may not open.

## A Practice, Not a Gift

None of this is a gift some are born with and others lack. It is a practice, and
practices are learned by doing them badly and then less badly. I would set down,
for the beginner, a short rule of life:

First, write the thing however it comes, crooked and overdressed, and do not
scold yourself for the mess. The mess is the ore. Second, read it aloud, and
strike out every word that the saying of it proves you did not need. Third, do
this again the next day, when the warmth of having written has cooled and you can
see the page as a stranger sees it. What survives three cold readings is usually
worth keeping.

The labor is real, and I will not pretend otherwise. But it is the kind of labor
that pays the laborer twice: once in the clearer page, and once in the clearer
head that made it. For you cannot, in the end, write more plainly than you think.
The plain sentence is the honest confession of a mind that has done its work.

## Conclusion

So let the words be plain, and let them be few, and let each one be held to the
light before it is spent. The reader you will never meet is depending on it. He
has given you the one thing he cannot get back, which is his attention, and he
has asked, in return, only to be told the truth in words he can carry. It is not
a hard bargain. It is only a demanding one — and the demand, rightly met, is the
whole of the craft.
