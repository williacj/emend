# Collected Discourses

*A gathering of short addresses on work, patience, and the ordinary day. The
pieces were written to be read one at a time, in any order, as the reader has
appetite for them.*

This volume collects the addresses into numbered books. The same handful of
themes returns in each book, taken up again from a slightly different side — for
these are not arguments to be won once and set down, but habits to be practiced,
and a habit is learned by returning to it.

---

## Book I

### I.1 — On Honest Work

Honest work is work that would not be ashamed to be examined. It does not depend on
the inspector's ignorance. It is the same all the way through, at the seam that
shows and the seam that does not, because the one who made it could not bring
himself to make it otherwise.

There is a false economy that dresses itself as cleverness: do only what is seen,
skimp where the skimping will not be caught, and pocket the difference. It works,
for a while. But shoddy work has a way of announcing itself in its own time, long
after the maker has been paid and gone, and the announcement is made in his name.

> Your work is a letter you sign whether you mean to or not. Long after you have
> forgotten a thing you made, it is out in the world, saying what you thought was
> good enough.

So make the unseen seam as true as the seen one. Not because you will be caught —
you may not be — but because the work is a confession, and it is better to have
nothing to confess.

### I.2 — On Patience

Patience is badly taught, because it is taught as waiting, and it is not waiting.
The one who waits does nothing and calls it virtue. The patient one does the slow
thing on purpose, at its proper pace, refusing to force a growth that will not be
forced.

Consider the difference between a fruit ripened on the branch and one ripened in a
crate. Both are yellow; only one is sweet. Haste can produce the color of a
finished thing without its substance, and the untrained eye is fooled, and the
tongue is not.

> Everything worth having has a season, and the season cannot be argued with. You
> may plant early or late, but you may not harvest early. The field keeps its own
> clock and does not consult yours.

So learn the pace of the thing you are making, and match it, and do not mistake
your impatience for diligence. They wear the same coat and keep very different
company.

### I.3 — On the Small Task

We despise the small task and are ruined by the contempt. The letter unanswered,
the tool unreturned, the promise kept *mostly* — none of these seems large enough
to matter, and that is exactly how they do their damage: below the line where we
bother to look.

A life is not built from a few great acts, however much we would prefer it. It is
built from the ten thousand small ones, most of them done or left undone before
breakfast. The person who is faithful in the small thing has, without noticing,
been practicing for the large one; and the person who is careless in the small
thing has been practicing too, for the opposite.

- The small task is where character is actually kept, or lost.
- No one is watching the small task, which is precisely what makes it a test.
- Do the small thing as if it were the large one, and the large one, when it
  comes, will find your hands already trained.

> Whoever cannot be trusted to sweep a floor should not be surprised to be passed
> over for the building of a house.

### I.4 — On the Given Day

Each morning hands you a single day and asks nothing in return but that you spend
it. You did not earn it and cannot keep it; by nightfall it is gone, whatever you
have done, and another is not promised.

This ought to sober us and instead it rarely does, because the days come so
regularly that we treat them as infinite. But a thing given daily is not thereby
given endlessly. The stream runs, and runs, and then one day it does not, and the
one who counted on tomorrow discovers that tomorrow was always a loan and never a
possession.

> Number your days, not to darken them, but to weigh them — for a thing counted is
> a thing valued, and a thing valued is spent with more care than a thing assumed.

I do not say live each day as if it were your last; that is a counsel of panic and
produces bad work. I say live each day as if it were your *only* one, which it very
nearly is, and give it the attention you would give a thing that will not come
again — because it will not.

### I.5 — On Diligence

Diligence is not speed. The hurried worker and the diligent worker may finish on
the same afternoon, but they do not finish the same work. Speed asks only *when*;
diligence asks *how well*, and keeps asking after the answer has stopped being
interesting.

The mark of a diligent hand is that it returns. It comes back to the joint that
almost fits, the sentence that nearly says it, the sum that balances if you do
not look too closely — and it does the small, unglamorous thing of looking
closely.

> The world is not undone by great failures of will. It is worn down, quietly, by
> a thousand things done *nearly* well by people who were almost paying attention.

Three counsels, then, for the diligent:

- Finish the part no one will inspect as carefully as the part they will.
- Distrust the work that pleased you too quickly; it usually flattered you.
- When you are tired, stop — but stop at a place you can honestly return to.

### I.6 — On the Unseen Reader

You will never meet most of the people your work will reach. This is easy to forget
at the desk, where the work feels private, a conversation between you and the page.
It is not private. The page is a door, and strangers will come through it whom you
cannot see and cannot answer.

Write, then, for the one who is not in the room. He cannot ask you what you meant;
he has only what you wrote. He is tired, and busy, and generous enough to give you
his attention for exactly as long as you are worth it and not one moment more. He
is the truest audience precisely because he owes you nothing.

- He cannot hear your tone, so put the tone in the words.
- He cannot see your intent, so mean what the sentence says.
- He will not double back to rescue your meaning, so do not make him.

> The reader you respect is the reader you will never meet, because him you cannot
> charm — you can only serve.

Serve him well, and you will find you have served everyone; for the stranger's
plain need is the same need every reader has, only stripped of the manners that
let you off easy.

## Book XXXVII

### XXXVII.1 — On Diligence

Diligence is not speed. The hurried worker and the diligent worker may finish on
the same afternoon, but they do not finish the same work. Speed asks only *when*;
diligence asks *how well*, and keeps asking after the answer has stopped being
interesting.

The mark of a diligent hand is that it returns. It comes back to the joint that
almost fits, the sentence that nearly says it, the sum that balances if you do
not look too closely — and it does the small, unglamorous thing of looking
closely.

> The world is not undone by great failures of will. It is worn down, quietly, by
> a thousand things done *nearly* well by people who were almost paying attention.

Three counsels, then, for the diligent:

- Finish the part no one will inspect as carefully as the part they will.
- Distrust the work that pleased you too quickly; it usually flattered you.
- When you are tired, stop — but stop at a place you can honestly return to.

### XXXVII.2 — On Honest Work

Honest work is work that would not be ashamed to be examined. It does not depend on
the inspector's ignorance. It is the same all the way through, at the seam that
shows and the seam that does not, because the one who made it could not bring
himself to make it otherwise.

There is a false economy that dresses itself as cleverness: do only what is seen,
skimp where the skimping will not be caught, and pocket the difference. It works,
for a while. But shoddy work has a way of announcing itself in its own time, long
after the maker has been paid and gone, and the announcement is made in his name.

> Your work is a letter you sign whether you mean to or not. Long after you have
> forgotten a thing you made, it is out in the world, saying what you thought was
> good enough.

So make the unseen seam as true as the seen one. Not because you will be caught —
you may not be — but because the work is a confession, and it is better to have
nothing to confess.

### XXXVII.3 — On Patience

The one who waits does nothing and calls it virtue. The patient one does the slow

forced.

crate. Both are yellow; only one is sweet. Haste can produce the color of a
finished thing without its substance, and the untrained eye is fooled, and the
tongue is not.

> may plant early or late, but you may not harvest early. The field keeps its own
> clock and does not consult yours.

company.

### XXXVII.4 — On the Given Day

### XXXVII.5 — On the Unseen Reader

You will never meet most of the people your work will reach. This is easy to forget
at the desk, where the work feels private, a conversation between you and the page.
It is not private. The page is a door, and strangers will come through it whom you
cannot see and cannot answer.

he has only what you wrote. He is tired, and busy, and generous enough to give you

is the truest audience precisely because he owes you nothing.

- He cannot hear your tone, so put the tone in the words.
- He cannot see your intent, so mean what the sentence says.
- He will not double back to rescue your meaning, so do not make him.

> The reader you respect is the reader you will never meet, because him you cannot
> charm — you can only serve.

Serve him well, and you will find you have served everyone; for the stranger's
plain need is the same need every reader has, only stripped of the manners that
let you off easy.

### XXXVII.6 — On the Small Task
